#1 Sui Ishida

Ishida is most known for his series Tokyo Ghoul. Tokyo Ghoul is about a world where two kinds of people exist, man-eating people called ghouls, and standard humans. It follows a college student who develops a crush on a girl who frequents the same coffee shop as him. They go on a date and he discovers that she is a ghoul when she tries to eat him alive. Miraculously, she dies but he is in critical condition. A doctor saves him by putting her organs within our main character, Kaneki, to keep him alive. This turns him into a half-human half-ghoul mix. The rest of the story follows his journey in exploring this new world he's thrown into and discovering what it means to be truly human. I love Tokyo Ghoul. It is my favorite manga series of all time. Ishida has a unique way of portraying intense emotions through his artwork. You can genuinely feel the despair of the characters and the weight of the situations they are in. For example:

MINOR SPOILERS AHEAD FOR TOKYO GHOUL

At one point, Kaneki is put into a situation where he is at a man's mercy. The man brings two people to face Kaneki. A mother and child. The captor tells Kaneki that he must pick who dies. The mom pleads with Kaneki to save her child, but Kaneki, afraid to be responsible for either person's death sits and pleads with his captor to let them both go. He refused to choose. Instead, he insisted that if someone should die, it should be him. Essentially sacrificing himself, to save the mother and child. In response, the captor decides to kill both of them in front of Kaneki, by first, choking the child in front of the mother, then impaling the mother.

This scene was so powerful to read. It was such an uncomfortable moral dilemma which begs multiple questions to the readers. Who would you choose if put in the same situation? Who's to say that because you choose one, the other won't die? What kind of effects would that have on the other person? Or let's say that you miraculously save both of them by breaking free, would you be ok with killing another to save yourself and more? How does that make you any different from the captor in the first place? All of these moral dilemmas are evidently shown on Kaneki's face during this scene. It's clear that he's torn. But he has to make a choice. Or does he?

What he doesn't acknowledge at this point was that he was strong enough to break out and save both of them. He had the strength to fight back against his captor. But what he didn't have was the self-confidence or self-worth to believe that he should. His ghoulification coupled with his childhood trauma made him believe that he deserves less than everyone else. He believed that he deserved all of the pain coming his way. He believed in taking the pain for everyone else than hurting others because that's how he was raised. Only after this scene did he finally confront his pain and acknowledge how much he hated bearing it all for those around him. Only after giving into his own wants and becoming more selfless did he find the strength within him to break free. But he had gotten no stronger from before. He had just learned to stop waiting on someone to save him and to learn how to save himself. Instead of taking everyone's pain because he feels as though he's worthless, he built the resolve that he will instead take on the pain for those he cares about and dish it back out to anyone who threatens their safety. While this isn't a perfect mentality, it shows his progression from the timid book-worm, into the vindictive avenger.

What Ishida explores here is huge. If you haven't been able to pick up on it already, he's not just talking about fictional lives. Kaneki's moral dilemma is something we often find ourselves wrapped up in our every day lives. Imagine this, it's 11pm. You're tired, you have work/school in the morning and you're not even close to finishing the tasks that you're behind on. You know that your energy is spent. But then someone you're acquainted with calls you, and they ask for your help finishing a project, completing an assignment, or solving a personal problem. In this situation, you are faced with choosing between yourself and them. You have to choose one person to save. You could save them, but your life will crumble. Your deadlines will approach and you won't have the time you need to do what you need to do. You could say no to them and to your work, but then you risk your entire future fading into obsurity, same consequence. But what-if you instead choose to save yourself. Maybe if you save yourself, maybe you'll find the time to help them. Maybe you won't. At the end of the day however, if you prioritize someone else's well-being above yours, you will undoubtably throw your own future away. That's the message Ishida wanted to get across and believe it or not, this is a controversial take considering he is a Japanese man.

Japanese society is heavily based on self-sacrifice over self-preservation. Specifically, many mangaka work tirelessly to produce chapters on a weekly basis. Most workers seldomly get a full night's rest, time with their families, or time to do anything aside from work. This unhealthy culture has even driven many Japanese men to make a choice that they can't reverse. Ishida is making a commentary on this culture. He uses Kaneki to illustrate the line between kindness and self-erasure. Yes, it's kind to give to others but not at the expense of yourself. If you can't take care of yourself then, in the end, everyone loses.

This is just one scene from Tokyo Ghoul that touches on culturally revelant topics and I didn't even mention the commentary on racial relations. As you can see, I'm very passionate about the story telling of Tokyo Ghoul. As someone who is naturally more empathetic, I often give out more than I truly should. In fact, oftentimes, I enjoy to sacrifice my well-being if it means that my loved ones can live easier. But as the story continues to illustrate in Tokyo Ghoul and Tokyo Ghoul:RE, no matter how you try to frame it, sacrificing your well-being will never help either parties in the long run. That's why Kaneki, and by extension my, mentality are not stable enough for the real world. Sure it's enough to move forward but it won't hold for long. Kaneki's metamorphoses are deeply important to me because of the fact that I related so well. The more I analyze this story, the more I am able to learn more about myself and improve myself. For those reasons, along with the immaculate artistic work, Sui Ishida remains to be my favorite mangaka of all time.
